The problem

Once a satellite's AI decides, nobody on the ground can check it.

Onboard AI saves bandwidth by throwing data away and acts faster by raising alerts in orbit. What it saw, what it decided and whether the record was changed later are invisible. As insurance payouts, emissions reports and security decisions start to depend on those calls, "trust us" stops being enough.

How it works

A flight recorder for onboard AI.

  1. Record

    Each decision becomes a record: the image's fingerprint, the exact model weights, the score and the verdict.

  2. Sign the pass

    Records chain into a Merkle tree. At the end of the pass the satellite signs one small summary.

  3. Lock in alerts

    An alert is signed the moment it fires. The final pass must extend it, so history can't be rewritten afterwards, even by the key holder.

  4. Verify anywhere

    Anyone with the public key checks the pass, or any single image, kept or discarded, on their own machine.

  5. Re-run

    A second machine recomputes sampled decisions with the same model and flags any it wouldn't have made.

Standard cryptography throughout: SHA-256, RFC 6962 Merkle trees and Ed25519. It sits beside your existing models and leaves the radio link and its security to the platform.

AI assurance
The same engine proves any AI decision on any device. Orbit is simply the hardest place to do it.
What a pass check proves

What the satellite decided, on which image, with which model version, in what order, and that none of it changed afterwards.

What it doesn't prove

That the decision was right: a verified fire alert proves the AI said "fire", not that there was one. Wall-clock time needs a trusted time source, which isn't in place yet.
